Redesigning the back-office where retailers manage products, prices and campaigns

The problem: Store teams managed thousands of products and complex pricing in a back-office built by developers, with no design input and no user testing. The product form showed 40+ fields at once, and weighted campaigns were set up by hand.

What I did: Interviewed 6 managers from 6 customer businesses, ran two card-sorting sessions, and analysed three months of real campaign data. I redesigned the product form and campaign creation, working with the PM and engineering team.

Key decisions: Split the product form into three tiers based on card sorting. Replaced the manual campaign form with two templates, after finding that 94% of weighted campaigns followed two patterns.

Result: A seven-step inventory and pricing flow cut to two, 35% less operational friction, and campaign creation 25% faster with 78% fewer errors. The redesign is live in several stores.

Part of the Focalpay platform: products and prices set up here power the Cashier POS and the Mobile shopping app.

The challenge

The Focalpay Back-office is where retailers set up everything the POS and the shopping app use: products, prices and campaigns. Customers ranged from the footwear brand Icebug to supermarket chains like Matrebellerna and Matvärlden.

I joined halfway through. Developers had built the back-office without a designer, and it had never been tested with users. I focused on the two areas where store teams lost the most time and made the most mistakes: managing products and creating weighted campaigns.

I worked with the same PM and engineering team as on the mobile app, from interviews and card sorting to testing, a design system in light and dark mode, and handoff to engineering.

Research

I interviewed 6 managers from 6 customer businesses. They did real tasks while thinking aloud, and I followed up with questions. With a limited number of B2B customers, I could work directly with the businesses that used the product.

Process

Product hierarchy Campaign templates Old vs new evaluation

I audited the existing screens, ran two card-sorting sessions with 4 retail managers each, explored three layout ideas with the PM and engineers, and built mid-fidelity prototypes. Then I tested the old and new versions with 5 store managers and handed off component specs to engineering.

Campaign templates

94% of campaigns followed two patterns

The interviews told me creating campaigns was painful, but not why. So I asked our supermarket customers, the main users of weighted campaigns, for their last three months of campaign data, and analysed it myself.

94% of weighted campaigns followed one of two patterns. So instead of a flexible form where every campaign meant doing the maths, I designed a builder with two ready-made templates.

TemplateLogicUse Case
Template ABuy over X kg → pay Y SEK/kgNear-expiry items · Bulk purchase
Template BBuy under X kg → pay Y SEKPremium deli · Small-portion buyers

Using the store's words

In one session, a manager wasn't sure what "Base unit price" meant. Their business used a different term. Other users with similar businesses agreed: their everyday words, like "Bulk purchase deal", were clearer than field names from the database. So the builder uses their words.

Nobody walks into a deli asking for the base unit price.

Reflection

What I learned

01

Use the words your users use

Part of what made the template builder work was the vocabulary. It used the words store managers already used every day. When an interface uses database names, it feels like a tool for the system. When it uses the words from people's jobs, it feels like a tool for them.

02

Data shows what interviews can't

The interviews told me campaign creation was painful. Three months of campaign data showed me why, and what to build. Asking customers for their data took more effort than another workshop, but it turned a general complaint into a clear design decision.

03

Small changes can matter a lot. Moving one field to the top of a form doesn't look like much in a design review. In a busy store, it adds up every single day.
