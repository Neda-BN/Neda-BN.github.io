Introduction

The product

The Focalpay platform merges checkout, payments and back-office into one system. The Back-office is its control centre: the place where retailers, from single stores to chains, manage the products, stock, prices, campaigns and payment methods that every checkout uses. Customers ranged from the footwear brand Icebug to supermarket chains like Matrebellerna and Matvärlden.

Focalpay built it to support stores, empower staff and protect margins, by helping teams act early instead of reacting. That made two areas critical: knowing when stock runs low, and setting up campaigns correctly. In both, the system still left the remembering, the checking and the maths to store managers.

Problem 1 of 2

Replenishment

From empty shelves to early warnings

Introduction

The challenge

Store managers had no warning before a product ran out. They found out when the shelf was already empty, and then ordering took seven manual steps.

The one question

So every decision had to answer one question:

does the manager know about low stock before the shelf is empty?

Discover

What we found

In interviews, managers did real replenishment tasks while thinking aloud, and I followed up with questions. Two findings shaped the work:

  1. No warning before a product ran out. To prevent it, managers checked their best-selling products one by one, and still found out when the shelf was empty. — Interviews
  2. Ordering was done by hand, every time: finding the supplier, working out quantity and cost, and writing the order email. — Interviews
Connect

From findings to directions

  • No warning → alerts that sort themselves by urgency
  • Ordering by hand → orders with supplier, stock levels and cost filled in

Design

Three decisions

Before, ordering stock took seven steps, and the first one was a surprise.

Before · 7 steps

  1. 1Notice a product is empty on the shelfNo warning
  2. 2Check the stock for that product
  3. 3Review other best-selling products one by one
  4. 4Find the supplier and their contact details
  5. 5Work out the quantity and cost
  6. 6Write and send the order email
  7. 7Keep track of the delivery manually

After · 2 steps

  1. 1

    Open the alert

    Out-of-stock products, grouped by urgency.

  2. 2

    Place the order

    Supplier, stock levels and cost already filled in.

Deliveries are then tracked in the app, and overdue orders flag themselves.

Decision 1 · Alerts that sort themselves by urgency

All notifications live on one page, grouped and colour-coded: red for products out of stock, yellow for overdue deliveries, blue for products that still need stock rules. Each group says in one plain sentence what needs doing. Managers get less detail at first glance, but far less noise, and the details are one click away.

Notifications grouped by urgency, each with one clear next step.

Decision 2 · One overview, with stock rules in one place

The Replenishments page opens with four summary cards: Out of stock, Setup needed, Incoming deliveries and Shelf refill, so managers start from what needs them. Alerts only work if every product has a minimum and maximum stock, so products without them are collected in one list, where managers set them directly, in pieces or kilos.

Summary cards on top, every product's stock status below.

Setting stock rules for 15 products from one screen.

Decision 3 · Ordering and deliveries without the paperwork

From the Out of stock list, one tap opens the order, with stock levels at the top, the supplier's email filled in and the total cost calculated from the latest in-cost. Orders are sent by email straight from the app. Deliveries show how many days they're overdue, and managers confirm each one as received or partially received, with the invoice and a note.

Problem 2 of 2

Campaigns

From maths to templates

Introduction

The challenge

Weighted campaigns, for products sold by weight like fruit or deli items, were slow to set up and easy to get wrong.

The one question

So every decision had to answer one question:

can a manager set up a weighted campaign without doing the maths?

Discover

What we found

In the same interviews, managers set up campaigns while thinking aloud. Three findings shaped the work:

  1. Every weighted campaign meant doing the maths. Managers calculated each discount by hand and typed it in. — Interviews
  2. Prices and campaigns drifted apart. "Every time base prices change, I'm double-checking campaigns by hand." 4 of 6 managers reported inconsistencies between catalogue data and campaign rules. — Interviews
  3. The system spoke the database's language. A manager wasn't sure what "Base unit price" meant, and others with similar businesses agreed: their everyday words were clearer. — Interviews
Connect

From findings to directions

  • Maths by hand → ready-made templates for the most common campaigns
  • Double-checking by hand → the system knows each product's base price and calculates the rest
  • Database words → the store's own words
Turning point

The interviews said it was painful. The data showed the way out.

What managers told me

Campaigns take too long

What the data showed

94% follow just two patterns

Managers told me campaigns were slow, but not what most campaigns had in common. So I asked our supermarket customers for their last three months of campaign data and analysed it myself.

94% of weighted campaigns followed one of two patterns.

The aha moment

Managers didn't need a more flexible form. They needed the two campaigns they ran all the time, ready to use.

Design

Two decisions

Decision 1 · A three-step builder with two weight templates

Instead of a form where every campaign meant doing the maths, I designed a three-step builder: About → Terms → Products. The system already knows each product's base price, so in Terms the manager picks one of two weight templates, sets the weight and the campaign price, and the system calculates the rest. For example: over 2 kg for 30 SEK/kg, instead of the usual 45 SEK/kg. Templates are less flexible, but covering 94% of weighted campaigns in a few clicks was worth more than a form that could do everything, slowly.

Step 1: name the campaign; dates are optional.

Step 2: two weight templates, next to the regular campaign types.

TemplateTermsUse case
Template ABuy over X kg for Y SEKNear-expiry items · Bulk purchase
Template BBuy under X kg for Y SEKPremium deli · Small-portion buyers

Decision 2 · Using the store's words

So the builder uses the words stores already use, instead of field names from the database. Managers don't have to translate before they can act.

A live weight campaign, in plain words.

Results

Design system

One design system for both

I built a design system with a light and a dark theme. Both share the same components, spacing and interactions; only the colours change, so store teams can choose what works in their lighting.

Reflection

What I learned

  • Use the words your users use. One unclear field name can stop a manager. Plain store words removed the need to translate.
  • Alerts beat checklists. When the system warns early, managers act before the shelf is empty.
  • What I'd do differently: I'd ask for the campaign data at the start, alongside the interviews. The data showed the way out faster than another round of conversations could.
