Redesigning how retailers reorder stock and set up weighted campaigns

The problem: Store managers only found out a product had run out when the shelf was empty, and ordering took seven manual steps. Weighted campaigns were calculated and typed in by hand.

What I did: Joined midway as one of two designers and owned two flows, Replenishment and Campaigns, plus the design system. Interviewed 6 managers from 6 customer businesses, analysed three months of real campaign data, and ran workshops and tests with 5 store managers.

Key decisions: Alerts grouped by urgency, one overview to act from, orders with supplier and cost filled in, and two ready-made weight templates after finding that 94% of weighted campaigns followed two patterns.

Result: Ordering went from seven steps to two, with 35% less operational friction. Campaign creation became 25% faster with 78% fewer errors. The redesign is live in several stores.

Part of the Focalpay platform: products and prices set up here power the Cashier POS and the Mobile shopping app.

The challenge

The Focalpay Back-office is where retailers manage everything the POS and the shopping app use: products, stock, prices and campaigns. Customers ranged from the footwear brand Icebug to supermarket chains like Matrebellerna and Matvärlden.

I joined the project midway as one of two designers. I owned two flows, Replenishment and Campaigns, and the design system. When my colleague moved on, I became the only designer on the team, working with the PM and engineering.

Both flows had the same problem: the system left the remembering, the checking and the maths to store managers.

Research

I interviewed 6 managers from 6 customer businesses, covering both flows. They did real tasks while thinking aloud, and I followed up with questions.

Replenishment

Managers had no warning before a product ran out. To prevent it, they checked their best-selling products one by one.

"Every time base prices change, I'm double-checking campaigns by hand."

4 of 6 managers reported inconsistencies between catalogue data and campaign rules.
Campaigns

Weighted campaigns were set up by hand. Discounts on products sold by weight had to be calculated and typed in, which managers described as slow and easy to get wrong.

Replenishment: from empty shelves to early warnings

  5. 5Work out the quantity and cost

How I structured Replenishment

Incoming deliveries

Awaiting, with overdue warnings

Products to restock on the shelf

Alerts that sort themselves by urgency

All notifications live on one page, grouped and colour-coded: red for products out of stock, yellow for overdue deliveries, blue for products that still need stock rules. Each group says in one plain sentence what needs doing.

Trade-off

Grouping alerts means managers see "3 products out of stock" instead of three separate alerts. They get less detail at first glance, but far less noise, and the details are one click away.

One overview to start from

The Replenishments page opens with four summary cards: Out of stock, Setup needed, Incoming deliveries and Shelf refill. Managers start from what needs them, not from a list of thousands of products.

Stock rules, fixed in one place

Alerts only work if every product has a minimum and maximum stock. Products without them are collected in one list, where managers set current, min and max stock directly, in pieces or kilos, without opening each product.

Ordering without the paperwork

From the Out of stock list, one tap opens the order. Current, min and max stock and shelf amount sit at the top. The supplier's email fills in automatically, and the total cost is calculated from the latest in-cost. Managers can add a second order for another supplier, then send it by email straight from the app.

"Moving stock level to the top sounds like a small thing. For us, it saves 20 minutes a day."

— Assistant Manager, Matvärlden

Deliveries as they really arrive

Orders don't always arrive on time or complete. Incoming deliveries shows how many days an order is overdue, and managers confirm each delivery as received or partially received, with the invoice and a note attached.

Campaigns: from maths to templates

Part 2 · Weighted campaigns

Weighted campaigns, for products sold by weight like fruit or deli items, were set up by hand. Managers calculated each discount and typed it in.

The interviews told me this was painful, but not why. So I asked our supermarket customers for their last three months of campaign data and analysed it myself. 94% of weighted campaigns followed one of two patterns.

So instead of a form where every campaign meant doing the maths, I designed a three-step builder: About → Terms → Products. In Terms, managers choose a campaign type in plain words, including two ready-made weight templates. Each type shows only the fields it needs.

TemplateTermsUse case
Template ABuy over X kg for Y SEKNear-expiry items · Bulk purchase
Template BBuy under X kg for Y SEKPremium deli · Small-portion buyers

Using the store's words

In one session, a manager wasn't sure what "Base unit price" meant. Other users with similar businesses agreed: their everyday words were clearer than field names from the database. So the builder speaks the way stores talk.

Nobody walks into a deli asking for the base unit price.

Trade-off

Templates make the common case fast, but they're less flexible. Covering 94% of weighted campaigns in a few clicks was worth more than a form that could do everything, slowly.

Reflection

What I learned

01

Use the words your users use.

Part of what made the template builder work was the vocabulary. It used the words store managers already used every day. When an interface uses database names, it feels like a tool for the system. When it uses the words from people's jobs, it feels like a tool for them.

02

Data shows what interviews can't.

The interviews told me campaign creation was painful. Three months of campaign data showed me why, and what to build. Asking customers for their data took more effort than another workshop, but it turned a general complaint into a clear design decision.

03

Alerts beat checklists.

Managers shouldn't have to remember to check. When the system warns them early, they act before the shelf is empty.

04

Looking back

What I'd do differently.

I'd ask for the campaign data at the start, alongside the interviews, instead of after them. The data explained the problem much faster than another round of conversations could.
