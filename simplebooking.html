Simple Booking Accounting Application

Redesigning an accounting app so business owners can do their own bookkeeping, without an accountant

Ongoing · about 50% redesigned and implemented
My Role
Lead Product Designer
Company
Cyberdesign AB
Platform
Web application
Methods
Expert review · Workshops · In-context testing

The problem: A large accounting app, built by developers without a designer, had never been tested with users. Its core workflows were confusing, cluttered, and in places incomplete.

What I did: Reviewed the whole app, built a prioritised roadmap with the PM, and ran a workshop for each core area with all three of the startup's real users. I redesigned those areas and implemented the new UI myself with Claude Code.

Key decisions: Reorganised the Overview by urgency, added clear statuses across invoices, quotes and receipts, added search and filters to the journal, and split payroll registration into steps.

Status: About 50% of the app is redesigned and implemented. Formal testing of V2 is next.

Introduction

Simple Booking is a young B2B startup building an accounting platform for small and medium-sized businesses. When I joined, the product was already big: invoices, quotes, receipts, journal entries, payroll and more. Developers had built all of it without a designer, and nobody had tested it with users.

Right now, the startup has three real users. All of them run small or medium-sized companies in Stockholm. Because there were only three, I could work closely with every one of them.

Why the design matters for the business

Simple Booking's idea is that business owners can do their own accounting, including taxes and month-end tasks, for a low monthly price, instead of hiring an accountant. Later, the company also wants to add AI features that help owners understand their numbers and spot problems.

So if the app is hard to use, the business idea doesn't work. If an owner can't see whether an invoice is paid, or what needs doing this week, they'll hire an accountant instead. That's why I checked every design decision against one question: can someone without accounting knowledge do this alone?

My Role & Responsibilities

I lead the UX work, from reviewing the existing product to building the new UI. I work closely with the PM, who I made the roadmap with, and with the back-end team, who connect the front-end I build to the back-end.

How I decided what to fix first

The app was big and complicated, so I started by going through it myself and asking the PM how they saw it. Together we made a roadmap. We ranked each part by how important it was for the business and how badly it was affected by usability problems or bugs.

Six parts came first, because the product can't work without them: Overview, Invoices, Quotes, Receipts, Journal entries and Payroll. For each one, I ran a workshop with all three users. They did real tasks in their own work environment, and then told me how it went and how the app made them feel. In one session, I sat next to a user and watched him work, while he said out loud what he was thinking. Then I looked for patterns across all three users and turned them into design decisions.

  1. Expert review

    I went through the whole app and listed problems, gaps and bugs.

  2. Roadmap with the PM

    We ranked each part by business importance and how serious the problems were.

  3. Testing in real work

    All three users did real tasks in their own environment, and I watched one of them in person.

  4. Finding patterns

    I compared what the three users said and did, and turned it into design decisions.

What we found, and what I changed

The findings follow the order people use the app: they create and send invoices, handle quotes, upload receipts, find transactions in the journal, check the Overview for what to do next, and handle payroll. V1 is the original version, and V2 is my redesign.

Before: original, developer-built After: my redesign, implemented with Claude Code

Invoices

Creating invoices, sending them and marking them as paid is the first thing users do after logging in. In V1, the invoice list had no clear statuses, and no search or filters. So users couldn't quickly see which invoices were paid, or find a specific one.

  • No clear statuses → colour-coded status on every invoice
  • No way to find an invoice → search and filtering

Recurring invoices

Recurring invoices had the same problem. The setup form came first, and users couldn't see what would be sent next.

  • Setup form on top → expectations and upcoming schedule first
  • No upcoming view → next 30 days at a glance
  • Crowded row actions → actions only when needed

Quotes

The buttons on a quote didn't show users what to do next. All buttons looked equally important, and some statuses were missing. So users couldn't tell if a quote had been sent, accepted or declined.

  • Equal-weight buttons → one clear next action, with secondary actions de-emphasised
  • Unknown quote state → visible statuses: sent, accepted, declined
  • Confusing workflow → each step shows what comes next
  • Form hogs the page → new quote on demand

Receipts

The upload area was so big that users had to scroll to see their receipts. The receipt date was missing, even though users wanted to sort by it. And every status was the same grey colour.

  • Oversized upload area → compact upload, table visible straight away
  • No receipt date → date column with sorting
  • Grey statuses → colour-coded statuses that scan at a glance
  • One long list → pagination
  • Four actions per row → one clear review action each

Journal entries

Every transaction ends up in the journal: receipts, invoices, payslips, taxes and more. That makes it the longest list in the app. In V1, there was no search, no filters and no sorting, so finding one transaction meant scrolling through the whole page.

  • Endless scrolling → fuzzy search by any value from a transaction, such as an amount, a name or a reference
  • One unfiltered list → filters by transaction type
  • Fixed order → sorting by column
  • Hidden manual entry → clear Add journal button
  • All entries expanded → collapsible cards

Overview

After users had added invoices and receipts, the Overview showed their finances, but as one big block of repeated information. Users couldn't see what needed their attention first. So I organised the page by time:

  • Seven equal alerts → one "Needs your attention" list for overdue invoices, month-end closing and other urgent tasks
  • Repeated deadlines → a "Due soon" group, then "Later this month" for tasks that can wait
  • Numbers mixed into tasks → a separate financial snapshot of revenue and performance
  • No clear next step → one-click actions and plain-language advice on each item
  • No sense of timing → a calendar showing what needs attention now and what comes next

Payroll

Registering an employee meant filling in two long forms. During the workshop, one user stopped and asked:

"I filled in this option above. Why is the system asking me to fill it in again?"

— Workshop participant, Payroll session

No field was actually repeated. There were just so many fields that the form felt repetitive. So the problem wasn't the data. It was too much information at once. I split the registration into smaller steps, so users only see what they need at each point.

  • Two long forms → step-by-step registration
  • Felt repetitive → related fields grouped into one step each
  • No sense of progress → a step indicator shows where users are in the process
  • No overview → summary cards up front

Design system

One foundation for Figma and code

Before redesigning the screens one by one, I built a full design system for Simple Booking. I didn't start from zero. I kept the parts of the old design that worked and improved them. This saved time, and gave the new design a structure the product can grow with.

The design system also makes building with AI more reliable. Claude Code uses the same colours, text styles and components that I use in Figma, so the app looks and works the same everywhere, instead of every screen being a little different.

Building it with Claude Code

From Figma to a working front-end

I bridge the gap between design and code. I built the front-end myself with Claude Code, working directly in the existing product, without a front-end developer. Because Claude Code uses my design system, every screen matches the Figma design, and we get from design to working screens much faster.

Reflection

What I learned

Working with three users

With a young product, I couldn't test with many people. But working with every real user on every core part of the app gave me a deeper understanding than a bigger, quicker study might have.

"Feels repetitive" is a real problem

The payroll form didn't repeat a single field, but a user still felt it was asking the same questions twice. If something feels complicated to users, it needs fixing, even when the data is correct.

Building it myself keeps the design intact

Because I build the UI myself with Claude Code, using my design system, my design decisions make it all the way into the product.
