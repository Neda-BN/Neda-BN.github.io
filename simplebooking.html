Introduction

The challenge

Simple Booking makes a bold promise: a Swedish small business can do its own accounting, from invoices to VAT and year-end, without paying an accountant. The stakes are real: bookkeeping rules are strict, deadlines come with penalties, and most owners aren't accountants. Every time an owner gets stuck, they contact support or hire an accountant, and the promise breaks.

When I joined, the product was already big, but it was built around the database, not around people. There was no clear hierarchy, statuses were missing, some parts were unfinished, and the app expected users to know accounting.

The one question

So every decision had to answer one question:

can someone without accounting knowledge do this alone?

My role

  • Sole product designer, working side by side with the PM and the back-end team.
  • From an expert review and workshops to final screens.
  • I build the front-end myself with Claude Code.
  • I proposed and designed Adviser, an AI assistant.
Discover

What we found

I reviewed the whole app and ranked each part with the PM by business importance and how badly it was broken. Then I ran workshops and in-context testing with all three real users, small business owners in Stockholm, doing real tasks in their own work. Four findings shaped the work:

  1. Nothing told users what to do first. The Overview was one big block of repeated information. — Workshops · Overview
  2. Statuses were missing or all the same grey. Users had to read carefully to know if something was sent, paid or overdue. — Workshops · Invoices, Quotes, Receipts
  3. Long lists had no search or filters. Finding one transaction meant scrolling through everything. — In-context testing · Journal entries
  4. Long forms felt repetitive, even when nothing was repeated. "I filled in this option above. Why is the system asking me to fill it in again?" — Workshop · Payroll
Connect

From findings to principles

I turned the findings into five principles, and I check every new screen against them:

  • Show what matters first → from the Overview
  • Make status visible → from Invoices, Quotes and Receipts
  • Make things easy to find → from Journal entries and Invoices
  • One clear next step → from Quotes
  • Less at once → from Payroll

Turning point

When simpler screens weren't enough

The plan

Make the screens simpler

The shift

Never leave people alone with a question

As the redesign moved forward, the team kept talking about simplicity. But accounting can't be made simple by removing things: every feature was needed. Clearer screens would help people find things, but not understand them.

The idea came from my own first weeks. I was new, my manager was busy, and I kept wishing someone could answer my questions right away. When I have a question these days, I don't search first. I ask an AI. Our users were in the same position, just with more at stake.

The aha moment

Simplicity wasn't about showing less. It was about never leaving people alone with a question.

The team was focused on launching the MVP quickly, and Adviser meant more work. So I designed a prototype in two hours and showed them. Once they saw how many problems one assistant could solve, they were genuinely excited, and we decided to build it.

Design

Four decisions

Adviser came out of the turning point. The other three decisions made the core screens clearer, alongside it.

Decision 1 · An adviser people can trust

Accounting is the last place for an AI that guesses. Adviser answers from the owner's own numbers and never records anything without confirmation. I worked with the back-end team through many sessions to shape how it works, then designed it and built its front-end.

  • Answers from your own numbers: users ask in their own words and get answers from the company's real figures.
  • Takes you to the right place (My idea): every answer links to the page where you can act.
  • Flags what needs attention (My idea): missing receipts and upcoming deadlines appear before they become penalties.
  • Suggests, never acts alone: nothing is recorded until the user confirms.
  • Never stuck: when it can't help, it creates a support ticket with a short summary.
  • Speak instead of type (My idea): users talk, pause, and hear the answer read aloud.

Decision 2 · An Overview that starts with what needs attention

The Overview is organised by time: one "Needs your attention" list first, then "Due soon" and "Later this month", a separate financial snapshot, and one-click actions with plain-language advice.

Decision 3 · Statuses and next steps you can read at a glance

Invoicing is what owners do most often, so it had to feel easy. I designed the invoice's whole journey as colour-coded statuses. Before adding reminders, I looked at how competitors handle late payments and checked the idea with two users.

Quotes got one clear next action per row and a new flow: owners send a quote by email or as a link the customer can accept or decline without signing in.

Receipts got a compact upload, a date column and colour-coded statuses.

Decision 4 · Long lists and long forms, made manageable

Journal entries, the longest list in the app, got search by any value, filters, sorting and collapsible cards. Payroll's two long forms became three short steps with a progress indicator: more clicks, but fewer fields at a time.

Design system

Before redesigning screen by screen, I built a design system, keeping what worked and improving the rest. Claude Code uses the same colours, type and components as my Figma file, so every screen looks and behaves the same.

Reflection

What I learned

  • Three users can teach you a lot. Working with every real user on every core area went deeper than a bigger, quicker study would have.
  • "Feels repetitive" is a real problem. If something feels complicated, it needs fixing, even when the data is correct.
  • What I'd do differently: I'd add short sessions with a few new users, to check that the patterns hold as the product grows.
