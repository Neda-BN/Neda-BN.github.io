Mobile Customer Shopping App

Letting shoppers scan and pay with their own phone, without queueing at the till

My Role
Lead UX/UI Designer
Company
Focalpay AB · 2023–2024
Platform
iOS · Web (PWA)
Methods
Interviews · Survey · Usability testing

The problem: Shoppers had to choose between carrying a store scanner and standing in a queue. Both were real barriers, especially for parents and people with accessibility needs.

What I did: Led the design from research to launch, working with the PM and engineering team. I tested in office workshops and in a live store.

Key decisions: Made sign-up optional after 3 testers abandoned at registration. Added visual, haptic and audio scan feedback after testers couldn't tell whether a scan had worked.

Result: About 30% better checkout task performance in in-store testing with 5 participants. The app has launched; adoption is still early.

Part of the Focalpay platform: this app connects to the Cashier POS for cash payments and to the Back-office for products and prices.

Introduction

In Swedish grocery stores like ICA and Coop, self-scanning means picking up a handheld scanner at the entrance, carrying it around, and returning it at the end. The other option is the queue at the till. For a parent with a stroller, or someone shopping with a sensory-sensitive child, neither works well.

The Focalpay app turns the shopper's own phone into the scanner. Shoppers scan products as they go, then pay by scanning a QR code on the store wall, without queueing. If they want to pay with cash, one tap sends their cart to the cashier.

I led the design from competitor research and interviews to final screens and usability testing. I worked closely with the PM and engineering team, and the PM joined some of the testing sessions.

Why Focalpay built this

Self-scanning devices cost retailers money. They have to buy them, charge them, maintain them and replace them. Focalpay wanted to remove that cost by letting shoppers use their own phones. The app is also part of a bigger platform, together with the cashier POS and the back-office, so retailers don't have to connect separate systems themselves.

This gave us three constraints:

  • No new hardware. It had to work with just a QR code on the wall and the shopper's phone.
  • Cash had to work too. Not everyone pays by card, so the app needed a way to hand over to the cashier.
  • Stopping theft without slowing people down. The store needed to confirm payment at the exit, without making honest shoppers wait.

Because the platform runs in the cloud, it can also lower hardware and electricity costs compared with traditional checkouts.

Research

Understanding the problem

I started by looking at competitors. Then I checked the problem with shoppers before designing anything, and tested the design in two different settings.

The problem

What wasn't working

In-store checkout was designed around the store's systems, not the people using them. For some shoppers, that made it hard to finish their shopping at all.

01

Store scanners need a free hand

Handheld scanners need one hand and full attention. For a parent pushing a stroller, or someone reading a list on their phone, that's very hard.

02

Queues

For shoppers with sensory-sensitive children, chronic pain or little time, queueing isn't just annoying. It can be a reason to shop somewhere else.

03

No feedback when scanning

In a busy store, shoppers couldn't tell whether a scan had worked. Some scanned the same item twice. The app gave no clear confirmation.

04

No way past a bad barcode

When a barcode wouldn't scan, the app had no other option, and shoppers got stuck. We only found this in the real store; the workshop sessions didn't show it.

“We want something that just works. Our customers shouldn’t have to think about the checkout — it should just happen.”

— Store manager, research session. The same store manager also worked with us on the Cashier POS.

Two decisions I made

Decision 1: Making sign-up optional

The first version asked shoppers to create an account before checking out. In testing, three participants gave up as soon as they saw they had to sign up. They said they just wanted a faster way to pay.

I suggested moving sign-up to the end and making it optional. Now shoppers can check out as a guest with their card, and get the receipt by email. Shoppers who want an account, with purchase history and saved cards, can still create one. In the next round of testing, nobody gave up, and one participant chose to sign up at the end.

Decision 2: Making every scan clear

In testing, shoppers scanned a product and then stopped and looked at the screen, unsure if it had worked. Some scanned the same item twice. In a busy store, with noise, trolleys and children, a visual sign alone was easy to miss.

So I added three kinds of feedback after each scan:

  • Visual: the product moves into the cart.
  • Haptic: the phone vibrates briefly.
  • Sound: a short sound plays.

This follows the WCAG guidance that a change shouldn't rely on only one sense.

After this change, testers stopped pausing after each scan, and we didn't see any more double scans in testing. This was something we observed in the sessions, not a measured number.

What I designed

Designed for one free hand

I designed every screen around one idea: the phone in the shopper's hand should be the only device they need, even while they push a stroller, check a list and watch two children.

Camera-first home screen
Main flow

When the app opens, the camera is already on and the cart is visible. Scanning a product adds it to the cart straight away. The cart, the total and the main shortcuts are always one tap away. There's no separate scan mode; the whole home screen is for scanning.

Shortcuts
Add bag · Baked goods · Manual barcode entry

Three shortcuts are always on the home screen. Shoppers can add a bag without scanning. Baked goods, which often don't have a barcode, have their own category. Manual barcode entry came from usability testing: when a barcode was damaged, shoppers had no way forward in the first version, so we added a simple number keypad.

QR checkout from anywhere in the store
Checkout

To pay, shoppers tap "Go to payment" and scan the large QR code on the store wall. It can be scanned from up to 10 metres away, which we tested in a real store. So shoppers can pay without going to the checkout area at all.

Many ways to pay, including cash
Payment

The app supports Apple Pay, Swish, card and cash. Cash users aren't left out: one tap sends their cart to the nearest cashier, without scanning everything again. Sign-up is optional, so shoppers can pay as a guest and get the receipt by email.

Exit QR code
After payment

After paying, the receipt screen shows a QR code for the exit gate. Shoppers scan it on the way out, and the gate opens. The receipt is also saved in the purchase history, with the products, total and discounts.

Where it is now

The app has launched, but only a small number of shoppers use it so far. So the results above come from testing, not from real use at scale.

As more people use it, these are the things I'd track to see if the design works outside testing:

  • Guest checkout versus account creation
  • Checkout completion rate
  • How often manual barcode entry is used
  • Cash handovers to the cashier

Reflection

What I learned

01
Test in the real store

The problems with damaged barcodes and missing scan feedback only showed up in the store session. The workshop sessions didn't show them. Testing with real noise and time pressure gave us findings we couldn't get any other way.

02
Feedback needs more than one sense

In a noisy store, a visual confirmation alone is easy to miss. Adding vibration and sound is what made shoppers feel sure the scan had worked.

03
Ask for sign-up after the shopper sees the value

Asking people to sign up before they had used the app made three testers give up in one round. Making it optional, and offering it after checkout, kept accounts available for the people who want them, without blocking everyone else.
