Overview

The problem

Shoppers had to choose between carrying a store scanner and standing in a queue. Both were real barriers, especially for parents and people with accessibility needs.

What I did

Led the design from research to launch, working with the PM and engineering team. I tested in office workshops and in a live store before launch, and ran an A/B test with real shoppers after launch.

Key decisions

  • Two checkout paths after 3 testers abandoned forced sign-up: sign up and pay in the app, or skip sign-up and scan the checkout QR code at the cashier area
  • Added visual, haptic and audio scan feedback after finding that the app was too silent: shoppers couldn't tell whether a scan had worked

Introduction

The challenge

For retailers, the whole idea depends on one thing: shoppers finishing checkout on their own phone. Every shopper who gives up halfway ends up back in the queue, or leaves without paying for what's in their cart.

In Swedish grocery stores like ICA and Coop, self-scanning means carrying a store scanner around, or standing in the queue at the till. Focalpay's app turns the shopper's own phone into the scanner.

Shoppers scan as they go. Signed-in shoppers check out in the app, while guests scan the checkout QR code at the cashier area. Shoppers who want to pay with cash send their cart to the cashier.

It had to work with no new hardware, support cash, and let the store confirm payment at the exit without slowing honest shoppers down.

The one question

So every decision had to answer one question:

does the shopper finish checkout on their own phone?

My role

  • Sole designer, from competitor research and interviews to final screens.
  • Working closely with the PM and engineering; the PM joined some of the testing sessions.
  • Tested in office workshops and in a live store before launch, and ran an A/B test with real shoppers after launch.
Discover

What we found

I started with competitors, checked the problem with shoppers before designing anything, and tested in two very different settings. Four findings shaped the work:

  1. Shoppers didn't want an extra device. 5 of 8 had used store scanners and asked why they needed one when their phone could already scan. — Survey · 8 shoppers
  2. Queues cost too much time. For shoppers with sensory-sensitive children, chronic pain or little time, a queue can be a reason to shop elsewhere. — Survey and interviews
  3. A store scanner needs a free hand, which a parent pushing a stroller doesn't have. — Interviews · 5 shoppers
  4. Damaged barcodes left shoppers stuck, and only the real store showed it. — Usability testing · in-store session

Connect

From findings to directions

Each finding pointed to a design direction:

  • Store scanners need a free hand → the phone becomes the scanner, with the camera on from the start
  • Queues → a checkout QR code at the cashier area, scannable from up to 10 metres away
  • No way past a bad barcode → manual barcode entry and a Baked goods category
  • Forced sign-up → two paths: sign up and check out in the app, or skip it and scan the checkout QR code at the cashier area

Design

Four decisions

These four decisions shaped the core flow before launch.

Decision 1 · Four steps before the first scan

A short, skippable carousel explains the store flow in four plain steps: scan, pay, get the receipt and exit.

Decision 2 · A camera-first home for one free hand

When the app opens, the camera is already on and the cart is visible, so the phone is the only device a shopper needs. Shortcuts cover what scanning can't: a bag, a Baked goods category for items without barcodes, and manual barcode entry for damaged ones.

Decision 3 · Two ways to check out, no forced sign-up

The first version asked shoppers to create an account before they could shop, and three testers gave up right there. So I designed two paths: shoppers who sign up can check out straight from the app, while those who skip sign-up scan the checkout QR code at the cashier area to connect their cart to checkout. It can be scanned from up to 10 metres away. In the next round, nobody gave up, and one participant chose to sign up.

Guests scan the checkout QR code at the cashier area to continue to checkout.

Decision 4 · Payment, receipt and exit

Shoppers see a payment summary and pay with card, Apple Pay, Swish or cash; cash users send their cart to the nearest cashier with one tap. When payment is complete, the phone shows a barcode to open the exit gate, so the store can confirm payment without slowing honest shoppers down. The receipt and every past purchase are saved in the app.

Turning point

The scanner wasn't the problem. The silence was.

My first guess

The scanner is too slow

What testing showed

The app is too silent

After launch, I reviewed shopper behaviour with my PM. Many shoppers started scanning but never completed checkout. My first guess was the scanner, so the dev team optimised it, but the drop-off didn't go away.

So I went back to testing, and found the real problem: shoppers scanned a product, then stared at the screen, unsure if it had worked. Some scanned twice. With noise, trolleys and children around, a visual sign alone was easy to miss.

The aha moment

Shoppers didn't need a faster scanner. They needed to know the scan had worked.

So every scan now gives three kinds of feedback: the product moves into the cart, the phone vibrates and a short sound plays, following the WCAG guidance that a change shouldn't rely on one sense.

In an A/B test after launch, with 10 real shoppers (5 on the silent version, 5 with feedback), checkout completion was 40% higher.

Results

What changed

Before launch, I tested the app in office workshops and in a real store. After launch, shoppers were giving up while scanning, which led to the scan feedback in the turning point, and an A/B test with 10 real shoppers measured its effect.

Reflection

What I learned

  • A noisy store is the best test lab. Damaged barcodes only showed up with real products, noise and time pressure.
  • Feedback needs more than one sense. Vibration and sound are what made shoppers sure a scan had worked.
  • What I'd do differently: My first guess was the scanner, and the dev team optimised it before we knew why shoppers were dropping off. Next time, I'd test first and fix second.
